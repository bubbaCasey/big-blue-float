    Questions to explore

    1. Temperature barely changes in the top 40–50 m or so, then drops. What mixes this surface layer, and about how thick is it?
    2. Chlorophyll is low at the surface but peaks near 110 m. Why would phytoplankton grow best so far below the sunlit surface? Compare the chlorophyll and nitrate profiles to build your argument.
    3. Oxygen drops below the chlorophyll peak, reaches a low near 200 m, then rises again around 600 m. What processes remove oxygen at mid-depths, and what might explain the higher oxygen deeper down?
    4. pH falls from about 8.04 at the surface to about 7.86 at 1,500 m. Using the oxygen and nitrate profiles, explain how decomposition of sinking organic matter could lower pH at depth.
    5. The light sensor reads essentially zero at every depth. Look at the local time of the profile. Why?
    6. Particle backscatter (a proxy for particles in the water) peaks a little deeper than chlorophyll. What kinds of particles might it be detecting?

      Questions to explore

      1. Check the drift distance at the top of this tab. Is the change you see in the upper 200 m more likely to come from the float moving or from the ocean changing between dives? What evidence supports your answer?
      2. Did the surface mixed layer get deeper or shallower? What weather would cause that change? (Hint: think about wind, heating, and the season in the Southern Hemisphere.)
      3. The chlorophyll peak changed in both depth and strength. Compare it with where nitrate starts to rise in each profile. How are the two linked?
      4. Below 600 m the profiles nearly match. Why does deep water change so much more slowly than surface water?
      5. Find one difference that could be sensor noise rather than a real change. How would you decide?

      A note on data quality

      These are raw, real-time data. The data center still marks oxygen, nitrate, chlorophyll, and pH as unadjusted because those sensors haven't been checked against reference measurements yet. The shapes of the profiles are reliable, but exact values may shift once corrections are applied. Temperature, salinity, pressure, and backscatter are good.

      Two things to watch: surface oxygen saturation reads about 92% when well-mixed surface water is normally near 100%, and surface nitrate reads about 1.2 µmol/kg in water that is usually almost nitrate-free. Both are typical raw-sensor offsets on a new float. Because the same offset appears in every profile, comparing one profile with another is still meaningful.
